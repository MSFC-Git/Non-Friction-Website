    What we believe

    Six things we've come to believe
    about how a business actually gets better.

    In the order they matter. Open any one of them for the longer answer.

    01 The most expensive thing in your business never appears on a supplier's invoice.

    Manual entry. Siloed information. Incomplete systems. Double entry. Nobody adds up the cost of all the inefficiencies in your business, because nobody ever sends you a bill for them. But they are there, they compound, and they're paid for out of your margin, your cash and your weekends. And that's only the part you can see.

    02 The biggest loss isn't to this year's profit. It's to what your business is worth on the day you sell it.

    Margin is the visible cost. The bigger one is what's left when you want out. Given the choice of selling your business for $100,000 or $1,000,000, which would you choose? A large part of that difference comes down to how much friction you left in the business.

    When the process is documented, the data is trustworthy and the reporting runs itself, a buyer is purchasing an asset that keeps running after you walk out the door. When none of that is true, they're purchasing a job — and they'll price it that way, if they bid at all.

    03 The friction in your business hasn't changed. The cost of removing it just collapsed.

    None of this is new. The reason it never got fixed is that fixing it properly meant custom work, and custom work meant an expert who understood both your business and the technology. Slow to find, slower to brief, and expensive either way.

    What's changed is that the same work can now be specified in plain English by the person who actually understands the business — you — and built in hours rather than months. Nothing about the friction changed. The price of removing it did.

    04 It's not about what you spend. It's about what you get back, and when.

    Which is what makes the sums work now, where they didn't before. If a change can't show a return, it isn't an investment — it's just another cost. Every improvement should pay for itself, and you should be able to see that clearly before you commit.

    05 Technology shouldn't replace people. It should replace the hours competent people lose to work they should never have been doing.

    Your project manager didn't learn the trade so they could type the same job details into two systems. Your administrator isn't there to chase a timesheet for the third time. Tasks like these have to happen, but not one of them is where the person's expertise lives.

    Take the friction away, and nothing of value leaves the business. The same people simply do more of the work you actually hired them for.

    06 Small improvements that hold will beat a heroic overhaul that decays. Every single time.

    Kaizen is the Japanese manufacturing discipline of continuous improvement — small, relentless and permanent, rather than occasional and heroic. A one-off overhaul decays the moment the business changes, and businesses change constantly.

    So removing friction works best as a standing discipline rather than a project. Where is your biggest friction occurring? Fix it. Where's the next one? Rinse and repeat.

    The Way We Think

    The Non-Friction Business.

    In chapter thirteen of Ernest Hemingway's The Sun Also Rises, Bill asks Mike how he went bankrupt. “Two ways,” Mike says. “Gradually and then suddenly.”

    That line has been borrowed to describe everything from divorce to ageing to the movement of tectonic plates. It fits business better than any of them.

    Businesses accumulate friction. Gradually.

    • The quote that took four days to go out instead of one.
    • The variation nobody wrote down.
    • The timesheet entered a fortnight late.
    • The job that looked profitable right up until it wasn't.
    • The invoice that sat unsent for three weeks.
    • The report you meant to build and never did.

    Not one of those is fatal. Together, they bleed a business dry.

    Friction creates drag. Drag consumes energy that could have produced forward movement. In a business, that energy is your margin, your cash and your weekends.

    It never gets fixed because each instance looks too small to be worth today's time and money. And it never arrives as an invoice, so nobody adds it up.

    Gradually, then suddenly runs in both directions.

    The same compounding that quietly bleeds a business will just as quietly build one. That is the whole idea behind kaizen — “change for the better” — the Japanese manufacturing discipline of small, relentless improvement instead of the occasional heroic overhaul. Toyota built one of the world's great industrial businesses on it. It transfers to a ten-person business almost without modification.

    It needs four things: a vision, a strategy, a plan, and action steps that actually get done. Weeks, months and years. Not a long weekend.

    So why doesn't every small business already do this? Because until very recently, fixing friction often didn't pay. Removing friction properly meant custom work, and custom work meant a developer — slow, expensive, and needing to be specified by someone who understood both your business and the technology.

    That has changed. The cost of the underlying intelligence has collapsed, and more importantly, the specification can now be done by the person who understands the business rather than the person who only understands the technology.

    A friction point that cost $20,000 to fix and saved $4,000 a year was a five-year payback and an easy “No”. At $2,000 it's a six-month payback and an obvious “Yes”.

    Nothing about the friction changed. The price of removing it did.

    That is why 2026 is different.

    The bad news

    In chapter thirteen of Ernest Hemingway's The Sun Also Rises, Bill asks Mike how he went bankrupt. “Two ways,” Mike says. “Gradually and then suddenly.”

    Gradually, then suddenly has since become shorthand for the way change, failure, growth and disruption actually happen in complex systems. It has been borrowed to describe everything from divorce to ageing to the movement of tectonic plates.

    Applied to business, it describes the way a company accumulates operational friction.

    • The quote that took four days to go out instead of one.
    • The variation nobody wrote down.
    • The timesheet entered a fortnight late.
    • The job that looked profitable right up until it wasn't.
    • The invoice that sat unsent for three weeks.
    • The report the owner meant to run but never had time to build.

    Not one of those cuts is fatal. Collectively, they bleed a business dry.

    In mechanical terms, friction creates drag. Drag consumes energy that could have produced forward momentum. In a business, that energy is your margin, your cash and your weekends — and ultimately the million dollars you could have sold the business for, but didn't, because you never removed the friction.

    Friction doesn't get fixed because each individual instance looks too small to justify the time, effort and cost of dealing with it. The return on investment looks poor next to whatever is on fire this morning.

    But all friction has a cost. It never appears on an invoice, so nobody adds it up.

    And so management tolerates it. Then tolerates it again. Then it becomes just how we do things here.

    The good news

    Gradually, then suddenly runs in both directions.

    We usually attach it to decay — entropy, depreciation, the slow slide. It applies just as well to growth, to success, and to the compounding of improvement.

    Albert Einstein probably never said that compound interest is the eighth wonder of the world. It doesn't matter. What matters is the idea underneath it: compounding is what turns gradual change into dramatic outcomes.

    The Japanese manufacturing world turned that idea into a method and called it kaizen — literally “change for the better”. Not a programme. A permanent cultural commitment to small, relentless, incremental improvement rather than occasional heroic overhauls.

    Toyota built one of the world's great industrial businesses on it. The good news is that it transfers to a ten-person business almost without modification.

    Kaizen requires four things.

    • A vision. What does this business look like when it runs properly? Be specific. “I know every job's margin the day it finishes” is a vision. “Be more efficient” is a wish.
    • A strategy. Which friction goes first, and why? Usually the friction that costs the most, or the friction hiding the information you need to make decisions.
    • A plan. A sequenced list with dates against it. Not everything at once.
    • Action steps. Small, specific, assigned, and actually done. Every week, not every quarter.

    The timeframe is weeks, months and years. Not a long weekend.

    This is not a project. It's a permanent change in philosophy, built on one idea: small improvements that compound and hold will beat a heroic overhaul that decays. Every single time.

    Why nobody does it

    None of this is new thinking. Continuous improvement has been well understood for forty years or more. So why don't more small businesses do it?

    Because until very recently, fixing friction often didn't pay.

    Removing friction usually meant custom work — off the shelf seldom fitted well. Integrating two systems that didn't talk to each other. Building the report nobody's software produced. Automating an approval. Restructuring how job data was captured in the first place.

    That was developer work. And developer work was slow, with endless alignment meetings. It was expensive. And it had to be specified by someone who understood both the business and the technology — a person who is genuinely hard to find.

    The return on investment (ROI) was real, but distant, and every bit of the risk sat with the owner.

    So it didn't get done. Owners weren't being short-sighted — they were just doing their sums correctly.

    What has changed in 2026

    Artificial intelligence (AI). Gradually, then suddenly. Sounds familiar.

    After a decade or more of promises, somewhere between 2025 and early 2026 we passed an inflection point that changed the return on investment equation. The cost of removing friction collapsed. Time, effort and expertise are no longer too expensive to justify.

    The clearest published measure comes from Stanford University's AI Index. Querying an AI model performing at the level of GPT-3.5 cost US$20.00 per million tokens in November 2022. By October 2024 it cost US$0.07. A more than 280-fold reduction, in roughly eighteen months.

    A million tokens is roughly 750,000 words — longer than War and Peace, or about eight ordinary novels. For seven cents.

    Put that reduction against something physical. A new Tesla Model 3 lists in New Zealand at NZ$63,900. Apply the same 280-fold drop and you would drive it away for NZ$228.

    That is not a discount. It is a different category of pricing.

    But the raw cost of intelligence is only the beginning. The bigger change is that work which used to require a developer — writing the integration, building the report, wiring one system into another — can now be specified in plain English and produced in hours instead of months.

    And this is the part that matters most.

    The specification can now be done by the person who understands the business, rather than the person who only understands the technology.

    The impossible-to-find translator is no longer the bottleneck. The technology person is still no better placed to understand your business than they ever were — they just aren't the gatekeeper any more.

    Building the thing no longer dominates the budget. Which flips the arithmetic entirely.

    A friction point that cost $20,000 to fix and saved $4,000 a year was a five-year payback and an easy “No”. At $2,000 it's a six-month payback and an obvious “Yes”.

    Nothing about the friction changed. The price of removing it did. That is why 2026 is different.

    The solution

    You cannot fix what you have stopped noticing. Which is why friction has to be systematically identified, not casually noticed.

    That means walking the whole path a job takes through your business — enquiry, quote, acceptance, scheduling, delivery, variation, invoice, payment, review — and asking at each step:

    • Where does information get typed twice?
    • Where does work sit and wait?
    • Where do we rely on somebody remembering?
    • Where do we not know the answer until it's too late to act on it?

    And a hundred other questions.

    Then you fix one. Verify a month later that it stayed fixed. Then do it again.

    That last part is not optional. Software bought without a vision, a strategy and a plan is just a new subscription. The return comes from the discipline, not the purchase.

    The ultimate prize — a business worth selling

    At first this is about avoiding failure. Failure can be an excellent teacher, but it's a long road back from bankruptcy and not everyone makes it.

    Then it's about surviving — earning enough to pay the bills and provide for your family. This is the business as a job.

    Beyond that comes growth: revenue, a capable team, systems and processes that hold. Then freedom. A business no longer entirely dependent on its founder.

    And at the highest level, the business becomes an asset in its own right. Valuable. Transferable. Able to operate without you. Generating wealth for its owners rather than wages for its founder.

    Which finally gives you the choice most owners never get: keep building it, sell it, or hand it on to the next generation.

    The key takeaway

    The businesses that treat friction removal as a standing discipline will pull away from the ones that don't. Not dramatically. Not this quarter.

    But a business improving a few percent every month, permanently, is a fundamentally different proposition in three years to one still running the processes it ran in 2019.

    That gap used to be hard to open, because the cost of improvement was high and the payback was long.

    That excuse is gone.

    What friction actually looks like

    None of this looks like a problem.

    A business can be busy, profitable and reasonably well run, and still be carrying an enormous amount of friction. It hides in the ordinary.

    Someone re-types information that already exists somewhere else.
    A quote gets lost between the person who sold it and the people who have to deliver it.
    Nobody is quite sure what happens after a job is won.
    Friday afternoon disappears into assembling reports.
    You answer questions the system should be answering.
    People keep private spreadsheets because the real system won't give them what they need.
    Invoices go out late because information hasn't reached accounts yet.
    Nobody can say for certain which version of the document is current.
    Staff have quietly invented their own workarounds.
    One person knows how everything actually works. Everyone else asks them.

    On its own, none of it is a crisis. Together, it's a full-time job nobody was hired to do.

    How we work

    Friction → Flow → Performance

    Three stages, in order. You can stop after any one of them and still be better off than you were.

    Stage 01

    Find the friction

    We follow the work, not the org chart. Where does time get stuck? Where does information get re-entered, lost or guessed at? Where does money sit still? You get a plain-English map of what's actually happening — which is usually not what the process document says.

    Stage 02

    Create the flow

    Redesign the steps so work moves through the business without being pushed. Sometimes that means changing the process. Sometimes it means configuring the software you already own so it fits the way you actually operate. Usually it's both.

    Stage 03

    Improve performance

    Connect the systems, automate the repetitive parts, and put reporting where people can reach it. Then keep going — because a business that's been fixed once starts collecting friction again the moment you stop looking.

    Where we differ

    Four things most consultants won't tell you.

    A well-designed business doesn't eliminate work. It eliminates unnecessary work.

    Anyone promising effortless is selling you something. People still have to sell, decide, manage customers and do the actual job. What should disappear is everything wrapped around that.

    Technology should remove friction, not create it.

    Plenty of software adds more work than it takes away — usually because it was bought to solve a problem nobody had defined. The tool is never the point.

    Sometimes the answer is don't buy another system.

    Fix the process you already have. We'd rather tell you that than sell you an implementation you didn't need. It's a shorter engagement and a better outcome.

    If your people are working around the system, the system is wrong.

    Workarounds aren't a training problem or an attitude problem. They're the most reliable diagnostic you have — staff route around friction the way water routes around a rock.
